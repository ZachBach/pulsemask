Assembly · printed parts to finished piece

Building it, one stage at a time.

You have the files, you have run the prints, and there is a tray of parts on the bench. This is the order they go together in, what to buy rather than print, and where the build actually gets difficult.

Eleven stages. Tick each one off as you go — this page remembers your progress in your own browser, so you can close it mid-build and come back. Nothing is uploaded and nothing is tracked; clearing your browser data clears the checklist too.

If you have not printed yet, start at the print & fit guide instead — portrait, IPD, orientation and material choices all live there.

Two absolute rules before you pick up a tool

Never fit a UV-C emitter. UV-C causes photokeratitis and skin injury, and the reactor cavity in this model has no interlock, no shielding survey and no line-of-sight verification behind it. Nothing sold here includes or supports emitters, drivers or batteries. If you want the piece to light, use EL wire or ordinary visible LEDs.

Never wear this for protection. Not for dust, not for fumes, not for solvents, not for pathogens, not for spray-painting the piece itself. It is a costume. Use real, certified protective equipment for real hazards — including the respirator you should be wearing at stage 03.

00 · Before you start

What you source, and what you never print

The model contains no fasteners. There are no bosses, no threads and no snap features anywhere in the geometry, and parts overlap rather than boolean-union — every mating face is an interference fit by construction. Plan on adhesive, and plan on sanding faces back until they seat. That is the single biggest expectation to set before you start.

Bench setup

  • A certified respirator and eye protection. Sanding SLS nylon and spraying primer both demand real PPE — the kind this project explicitly is not.
  • A heat source for the visor — a heat gun, or an oven you are willing to use for polycarbonate. It needs to form to a curve that runs in three dimensions.
  • A rotary tool with sanding drums, for opening interference fits without removing a whole face.
  • Masking tape — and more of it than you think. Every mating face gets masked before paint. There is no clearance to give back.
  • The exploded view, open on a second screen. Load the generator, click Exploded, and orbit it. That is the assembly drawing for this whole page.

Total build time: roughly 12–20 hours of hands-on work spread over four to seven days, most of which is curing and paint flash-off rather than labour. The first one always takes longer.

Stages 01–11

The build

Stages are ordered by access. Anything you can only reach from behind a surface goes in before that surface closes — which is why the visor beats the chin stack, and the interior beats the armour.

Build progress
0 / 11
01

Sort, inspect and dry-fit

Everything · before a drop of glue
1–2 h

Lay every part out in the arrangement the exploded view shows. Confirm you have all of them — a default build is 30 named meshes, and the ones people discover missing at stage 06 are the small interior pucks.

Then dry-fit the whole thing with tape. Every joint is interference by construction, so expect to open most of them. Sand the male face, never the female one: you can always take more off a spigot, but a bore you have opened too far is a shim job forever.

  • Check the shell's inner sealing face for support scars. This is the surface the bead beds onto, and a ridge there means a rocking seal you will never chase out later.
  • Look at the visor aperture edge for layer stepping — it is the most visible edge on the finished piece and the hardest to fix after paint.
  • Snap the thin parts now rather than at stage 07. The el_wire runs and the crest antenna are the fragile ones.
02

Surface preparation

shell_outer and every visible part
3–5 h

SLS PA12 arrives matt and slightly porous — bead-blast it, then filler-prime for anything above a satin finish. FDM parts want the layer lines sanded hard; the shell's 2.6 mm wall gives you plenty of material to do it with, which is one of the reasons the wall is that thick.

Work up through 220, 400 and 800 grit. The armour seam grooves and the panel crowns are the design's signature — a sanding block that bridges them will flatten the read, so cut those with the grain of the groove and finish them by hand.

Mask before you prime

Every mating face, the whole inner sealing face, the visor gasket seat and the four anchor slots get masked. Primer plus topcoat is easily 0.2 mm of build, and none of these joints has 0.2 mm to spare.

03

Paint and finish

Colour, sheen, spec codes
2–4 h + cure

Shell first, fully finished, before anything is bonded to it. Almost everything in the later stages either covers paintable surface or blocks access to it.

A Shader Lab spec code is a colour and finish reference, not a printing instruction. It swaps a material and never geometry, and only shell_polymer, bronze and cartridge_alloy accept one. Order PM1-HEXPLATE-85 and no hex plates appear in your STL — you get a colour and a sheen.

  • Shell — #dcd6c9 at roughness 0.58. Read that as satin.
  • Galea bronze — #9c7c46 at roughness 0.34. Semi-gloss.
  • Canister alloy — #8b9298 at roughness 0.33.

Roughness is a PBR parameter, not a gloss unit. Match it on a spray-out card under the light the piece will actually be seen in — a screen will lie to you about both value and sheen.

04

The visor

visor_gasket → visor_lens · from behind
2–3 h

This goes in early for one reason: you can still reach the aperture from inside the shell. Once the oronasal cup is in at stage 06, you cannot.

Cut the lens from 2.2 mm polycarbonate to the rounded-hexagon aperture — half-extents 56.5 × 29.2 mm, flat top and bottom, pointed at the temples. Trace it from the printed shell rather than from the mesh; your shell is the part that has to be matched, and it has your paint on it now.

That aperture edge curves in three dimensions, so a flat cut will not seat. Heat-form it over the shell itself or over a buck taken from it. Go slow and even — polycarbonate that is overheated locally will haze, and a hazed visor is a new piece of sheet.

Gasket into the aperture first, lens into the gasket second, both worked from behind. If you are using 4.8 mm cord instead of the printed gasket, cut it long, seat it around the full perimeter, and butt the ends at the bottom centreline where the join reads least.

05

The chin canister stack

Outward, in reverse airflow order
2–3 h

Seven parts, built outward from the shell in the reverse of the direction air would travel. Working this way means each part registers against one already fixed, and the grille — the only piece with a visible outer face — lands last and can be clocked to sit square.

  • canister_collar — onto the chin. This one sets the axis for all six that follow, so take your time squaring it.
  • pzt_blower_stack
  • uvc_reactor_housing — lathed at 8 segments. Those flats are design, not faceting artefacts; do not sand them round.
  • pzt_capsid_ring
  • filter_cartridge
  • pzt_coalescer
  • intake_grille — the cap, and the visible one.

Epoxy rather than CA here. The stack is the piece most likely to be knocked, it cantilevers off the chin, and a cyanoacrylate joint at that lever arm is a repair waiting to happen.

Also fit uv_status_band now if your set includes it. It is a status indicator, not a window — there is nothing behind it to see, by design.

06

The interior

oronasal_cup → seal_lip → pzt_seal_sensor
2–3 h

Cup before bead, always. The oronasal_cup is the thinnest structural part in the set at a 1.8 mm wall, and it needs to be positioned against the shell's inner surface while you still have clear access to both.

Then the seal bead — an 8.4 mm section tube following the 548 mm rim path. This part exists to be soft. If you printed it rigid, treat the print as a pattern: pull a silicone mould from it and cast the real one, or face it with closed-cell foam. A rigid bead against a face is the difference between a piece you wear for an evening and one you take off after ten minutes.

The pzt_seal_sensor pucks go in last, seated into the bead at their modelled positions. They are sculpt — solid ceramic-look parts with no cavity, no electrode and no wire route.

What the fit heatmap is and is not

The generator's fit view colours the bead by penetration into the reconstructed face. That surface came from a photograph, and the bead was placed at a fixed offset from it — so it conforms by construction. A green heatmap is a drawing agreeing with itself, not a measured seal.

07

The Galea layer

Scales, horns, PV, crest — onto finished paint
3–6 h

Only if your tier includes it. The armour layer is generated in place on the shell surface — it does not move in the exploded view because it never separates from the shell in the design. If your slicer handed you the plates as loose objects, they must go back to their modelled positions, which is why re-importing the full set into your slicer and using it as a placement reference beats eyeballing it.

  • Scale hood — around 160 plates at roughly 11 × 11 mm. Work in rows from the rim upward, alternate rows offset half a column, each plate overlapping the one below like roof tiles. This is the long stage and it is worth its own evening.
  • Solar horns — from the temples, curving up and back. Epoxy: they are the longest lever arm on the piece.
  • PV array — seven panels per horn on the outboard face. Cosmetic sculpt, no cells.
  • Crest keel and antenna — the crown centreline. The antenna is millimetre-scale detail; if it did not survive the print, the keel reads fine without it.
  • EL wire — thread the seam channels last, after everything they route around is fixed. Runs break automatically wherever they would cross an aperture, so follow the channels rather than trying to make one continuous loop.
08

Strap anchors and harness

Four points at 16°, 164°, 206°, 334°
1–2 h

The anchors are modelled as 26 × 12 mm loops swept at 2.7 mm, leaving a clear slot of roughly 20 × 6.6 mm. Bond them with epoxy — these carry the entire weight of the piece and they are the joint most likely to fail in use.

Thread a four-point harness: two lower straps around the back of the head below the occiput, two upper straps meeting in an over-crown strap. Skip the crown strap and the whole assembly rotates down your face as the chin stack pulls forward — which it will, because that is where the mass is.

Set the lower straps first with the piece held level, then take up the uppers until it stops rotating. Do not tighten to compress the bead; the seal is decorative and hauling on the straps to close it just puts pressure on your cheekbones.

09

Comfort facing

The stage everyone skips and regrets
1 h

Cut closed-cell foam to the 548 mm rim path and face the bead with it. Two to four millimetres is enough. Bare printed nylon against skin goes from fine to intolerable in about fifteen minutes, and it is the single most common reason a finished build sits on a shelf instead of getting worn.

Pay particular attention to the bridge of the nose and the point of the chin — the two places where the reconstructed surface is least reliable and where all the pressure concentrates. A second layer at those two spots costs nothing and buys hours.

10

Optional lighting

Visible spectrum only — read this twice
2–4 h

EL wire in the seam channels, or ordinary visible LEDs behind the status strip and the indicator band. Both read beautifully at night from the side, which is what the channel routing was drawn for.

There is no wire route, no cavity and no driver bay anywhere in the geometry — the horns, cells, PV panels, LED rings and PZT elements are all solid sculpt. Any cavity you need, you cut yourself, and you cut it before paint if you can plan that far ahead.

Not UV-C. Not ever.

The reactor cavity is drawn fully enclosed with no line of sight to the wearer, and that is the only reason it can be drawn at all. A physical build has no interlock, no leakage survey and no verified shielding. Fitting a UV-C emitter puts a documented eye and skin hazard millimetres from your face inside a part that was never tested to contain it. Nothing sold here includes or supports emitters, drivers or batteries.

11

Final pass

Before it leaves the bench
1 h
  • Run a fingertip around the entire visor aperture and the rim. Any edge sharp enough to notice is sharp enough to cut — break it.
  • Hang the piece by the harness and let it settle. Anything that shifts is a joint that has not cured or has not seated.
  • Check that no adhesive has wicked onto the visor. It will have. Now is the moment.
  • Photograph it before you wear it. Assembly marks show up fast, and the first hour is the best the finish will ever look.

Then send a photo to build@aureliusdynamic.com — builds get featured with credit and permission, and the problems you hit on the way are what improve this page for the next person.

Wearing it

Fitting the piece to your head

Photo-Fit generates the shell at your proportions, but proportion is not the same as fit. Landmarks set the size; photometric relief adds surface texture; neither recovers metric depth. Expect to adjust.

Put it on in this order

Straps fully slack. Chin into the lower rim first, then rotate the shell up onto the face — the same motion a full-face helmet takes. Take up the lower straps, then the crown strap, then even out the uppers. Trying to pull it on face-first fights the bead the whole way and drags foam out of position.

Where it will press

The bridge of the nose and the point of the chin, every time. Those are where the photographic reconstruction is least reliable, and where the whole mass ends up bearing. Add foam before you consider sanding the shell — the shell is finished and the foam is not.

Visor and vision

The aperture is drawn for a wide field, but a heat-formed lens can still distort at the temples. Wear it somewhere safe before you wear it somewhere crowded, and never wear it anywhere that needs peripheral vision — driving, cycling, machinery, a dark venue with stairs.

Breathing and time limits

The oronasal cup reduces the volume you rebreathe; it does not eliminate it, and modelled dead space is approximately 40 mL from an analytic estimate rather than a measurement. Take the piece off regularly. If you feel breathless, warm, or lightheaded, take it off immediately — that is the whole protocol.

Heat

A closed shell over the face is warm within minutes and hot under stage lighting. Nylon does not breathe. Plan short wears with breaks, not a whole evening sealed in.

Never as protection

Not for dust, fumes, solvents, smoke, spray paint or pathogens. It is untested to NIOSH 42 CFR 84, EN 136/143 and ISO 16900 — no protocol has been run against it at all. Wearing it where a real respirator is needed is more dangerous than wearing nothing, because it feels like protection.

After the build

Care, cleaning and storage

  • Clean the visor with warm soapy water and a soft cloth. No alcohol, no ammonia, no solvent glass cleaner — polycarbonate crazes, and a crazed visor is scrap.
  • Wipe the foam facing after every wear and let it dry fully in open air. It is the only part that touches skin.
  • Store it face up on a stand or on the printed face-form bust, never resting on the chin canister. That stack cantilevers, and gravity across a shelf will find the joint you rushed.
  • Keep it out of a hot car. PA12 softens and painted finishes bloom well below the temperature a parked car reaches in summer.
  • Out of direct sun for storage. Bronze and neon pigments both shift, and they shift unevenly, which is worse than shifting together.
  • Re-check the anchor bonds every few wears. They carry the load and they fail progressively rather than suddenly, so you get warning if you look for it.
When it goes wrong

Troubleshooting

Parts will not seat

Expected — everything is interference by construction. Sand the male face, test, repeat. If a joint is out by more than about a millimetre, suspect print shrinkage rather than the model, and check the same dimension on a second part before you remove material.

The visor will not lie flat

It is not supposed to. The aperture edge curves in three dimensions. Heat-form over the shell or a buck taken from it, in slow even passes. Local overheating hazes the sheet permanently.

The mask rotates down my face

The crown strap is missing or slack. Mass sits forward in the chin stack, so a two-point harness cannot hold it. Add the over-crown strap and set it before the uppers.

The bead hurts

It is printed rigid. Face it with closed-cell foam, or cast the bead in silicone from the printed pattern. Do not answer it by tightening the straps — the seal is decorative, and pressure is not fit.

The visor fogs

Some of it is the design's honest limit — the visor cavity in a physical build has no bleed sweep, because there is no blower. Anti-fog treatment on the inside face and short wears are the practical answers.

A thin part snapped

Usually the EL-wire runs, crest antenna or a scale plate. All three are the small-feature risk on any machine and all three are optional to the read. Re-print the individual part — each is a separate named mesh, so you can slice one without touching the rest.
